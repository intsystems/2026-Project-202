\section{Discussion}\label{sec:cost}
What MG reveals depends on which quantity we record. In the oscillating matrix, a single entry follows one rhythm. The norm combines all entries, and its MG rises as independent oscillations are added (Table~\ref{tab:matrix-observations}). During training, the interpretation also depends on what causes the change. In ResNet-18, measurements of the weight updates confirm that a drop in weight-norm MG accompanies motion in fewer directions (Figure~\ref{fig:resnet-freeze-trace}). For most estimator settings, weight-norm MG dips more deeply near generalization in the grokking runs than at the same steps in the controls. Removing the linear trend in each window eliminates this ordering (\appref{app:grokking}).

Whether higher or lower MG is useful depends on the task. On MNIST, encoders with higher gradient-norm MG tended to give better digit classifiers, and this ranking was useful on new training runs. For forecasting one neuron's activation, choosing a model by MG gave a similar mean error to choosing it by validation. For classifier interventions and transformer loss spikes, differences between successive readings detected at least as many events as MG at comparable false-alarm rates (Table~\ref{tab:decisions}).

\section[Conclusion]{Conclusion}
A scalar log can reveal simplification in recurrent activity at fixed weights and in a feedforward network's updates during training. Once independent measurements establish which change the log tracks, MG can help locate that change retrospectively in loss or weight-norm records from comparable runs.
