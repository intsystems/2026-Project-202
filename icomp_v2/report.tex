\begin{abstract}
Over a stretch of training, the parameter trajectory of a neural network stays on a set far smaller
than the parameter space. We call the dimension of that set the active dimension of the stretch.
Measuring it requires storing the trajectory, whereas a run writes scalar logs at no cost. We ask
how much of it can be recovered from one such scalar. We reconstruct a state space from the
scalar by delay coordinates and estimate the intrinsic dimension of that reconstruction by maximum
likelihood on nearest-neighbour distances. The procedure returns a plausible number even when the
trajectory never revisits any region of the parameter space, so most of the work is in establishing
when its output may be believed. On six constructed systems of known active dimension it is
accurate to about one component, up to a ceiling near eight. Statistics computed from the log
identify the records on which the value may not be read as a dimension at all. They reject both
standard grokking settings, so we store the trajectory instead. Its effective rank
collapses near the generalisation step in four regularised runs and then re-expands, the
simplification that mechanistic accounts of grokking predict; a run that has merely come to rest
falls as far: the sharpness and the reversal mark the transition, not the depth.
Re-run over a window as short as the transition, the estimate on an ordinary log falls at the same
step in those four runs and in neither of the other two, by more than randomised series that
preserve the shape of the log.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A network is trained in a parameter space of dimension $P$, but over a stretch of training the
parameters usually move within a far smaller set. Two open questions turn on that set.
Is an apparent simplification of training a real reduction in the number of degrees of freedom in
use, or an artefact of a shrinking scale? Can a phase transition such as delayed generalisation be
detected without instrumenting the run? Both are argued with whichever count of degrees of freedom
is least expensive to compute. The counts in use disagree: the \emph{available} dimension of the
subspace the parameters are confined to \citep{li2018measuring}, the \emph{functional} dimension of
the map from that subspace to the outputs, and the \emph{effective rank} of the trajectory
covariance.

Our two questions concern a fourth quantity: the number of independent components of the set the
parameters occupy. We call it the \emph{active dimension} of that stretch (\cref{eq:act}). If $r$ independent quasiperiodic phases
drive the motion, the path lies on an $r$-dimensional torus and the active dimension is $r$.
\Cref{sec:three} defines all four. \Cref{sec:digits} exhibits a change that moves the active
dimension while the other three stay where they are.

Measuring the active dimension means storing the parameter trajectory, which an ordinary run does
not do. A run does write scalar logs at no extra cost: a loss, a gradient norm, a parameter norm.
\emph{How much of the active dimension can be recovered from one such scalar?} Nonlinear time
series analysis supplies a standard answer: reconstruct a state space from the scalar by delay
coordinates \citep{takens1981detecting, sauer1991embedology}, then estimate the intrinsic dimension
of the reconstruction by maximum likelihood on nearest-neighbour distances \citep{levina2004maximum,
mackay2005comments}. \Cref{app:theory} states these results.

The procedure returns a number on any record. Power-law noise yields a finite and reproducible
value fixed by its spectral exponent \citep{osborne1989finite, theiler1991some}; a decaying
transient yields a large value. Establishing which case a training log falls in is the substance of
the problem. Most of the work below is spent on it, beginning with the validation protocol of
\cref{sec:protocol}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt, parsep=0pt]
\item We evaluate the estimate against a constructed ground truth on six systems of increasing
realism, ending with a linear head trained inside a $k$-dimensional subspace on top of a frozen
image network. On withheld ranks and seeds it recovers the active dimension to within about one
component, up to a ceiling near eight (\cref{sec:ladder}).
\item We establish the conditions the value depends on. The identifiability ratio and the
trend-crossing count identify the failure regimes from the series alone (\cref{sec:validity}).
\item We apply the procedure to grokking (\cref{sec:grokking}). Both published settings fall
outside the regime the estimate needs, so we store the trajectory instead. Its effective rank
collapses at generalisation and re-expands. Over a window as short as that transition the
estimate on a log declines with it in the four runs that generalise and in neither of the other two
(\cref{sec:matched}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reconstruction and intrinsic dimension.} Delay reconstruction follows from Takens'
theorem and its extensions (\cref{app:theory}), is used on empirical series \citep{sugihara1990nonlinear,
sugihara2012detecting}, and takes its lag and dimension from mutual information, false nearest
neighbours or singular spectra \citep{fraser1986independent, kennel1992determining,
cao1997practical, broomhead1986extracting}. We use pooled maximum likelihood
\citep{levina2004maximum, mackay2005comments}; the alternatives \citep{grassberger1983measuring,
facco2017estimating, camastra2016intrinsic} are bounded alike by a finite record
\citep{eckmann1992fundamental}. Oversampling \citep{theiler1986spurious} and power-law
noise \citep{osborne1989finite} produce spurious dimensions; surrogate tests
\citep{theiler1992testing, schreiber1996improved} detect nonlinear structure but not how much is
present and miss a decaying transient entirely.

\paragraph{Dimension in optimisation.} Random-subspace training \citep{li2018measuring,
aghajanyan2021intrinsic} reports the smallest $k$ that still solves a task, a property of the task
and not of a window; we adopt the same parameterisation but measure a different quantity within it.
Closest to the active dimension is the finding that descent occurs largely in a small subspace
spanned by the top Hessian eigenvectors \citep{gurari2018gradient, sagun2018empirical}.
\Citet{ansuini2019intrinsic} and \citet{pope2021intrinsic} estimate the intrinsic dimension of a
data set or of a layer's representations, which are properties of a point cloud, whereas the active
dimension is a property of a path. The participation ratio we adopt \citep{gao2017theory} is not
the spectral-entropy quantity of the same name \citep{roy2007effective}; it has been measured on a
grokking trajectory once, in passing \citep{notsawo2023predicting}. All of these quantities need
weights, gradients or activations, whereas the nearest precedent for a scalar computed during
training and acted on is the gradient noise scale \citep{mccandlish2018empirical}. Mini-batch
descent near a minimum is modelled as a stochastic process \citep{mandt2017stochastic};
\cref{app:eos} asks whether undriven descent at the edge of stability \citep{cohen2021gradient}
returns near states it has already visited.

\paragraph{Grokking.} Delayed generalisation \citep{power2022grokking} is studied in two settings
and we use both. The transformer setting is regularised and, in our version, stochastic: the
configuration of \citet{nanda2023progress}, also used by \citet{liu2022omnigrok}, on modular
addition and on the $S_5$ task of \citet{power2022grokking}. The perceptron setting is the
unregularised full-batch gradient descent of \citet{gromov2023grokking}, on the modular polynomials
and representability criterion of \citet{doshi2024grokking}, which supply label-matched pairs
(\cref{app:runs}). Explanations are framed in weight or activation space
\citep{nanda2023progress, varma2023explaining, liu2022towards, barak2022hidden} or in a
lazy-to-rich transition \citep{kumar2024grokking}. Closest to us, \citet{notsawo2023predicting}
predict grokking from early loss oscillations \citep{thilak2022slingshot} and
\citet{merrill2023tale} track competing subnetworks. We differ from all of these in estimating a
dynamical quantity whose ground truth is measured independently.

\section{Problem statement}\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{The active dimension}\label{sec:three}

A \emph{window} $\gW$ is a block of $L$ consecutive optimiser steps. Every quantity below is defined
on a single window. A run is described by a sequence of them. Let $\vtheta_t \in \R^P$ be
the parameter vector at step $t$, confined by construction or by the dynamics to an affine subspace
$\vtheta = \vtheta_0 + \mV^{\top}\vc$ with $\mV \in \R^{k \times P}$ orthonormal, so that
$\vc_t \in \R^k$ holds the free coordinates.

We call $k$ the \emph{available} dimension. The \emph{functional} dimension is the rank of
$\partial \vf(\vc)/\partial \vc$, with $\vf$ the outputs on a fixed probe set; it bounds how many of
the $k$ directions can change the computed function. The \emph{active} dimension is the subject of
this paper: the number of independent directions along which $\vc_t$ moves within the window. Let
$\gM_{\gW} = \{\vc_t : t \in \gW\}$ be the set of points it occupies there. The active dimension is
the box-counting dimension of $\gM_{\gW}$ at resolution $\varepsilon$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:act}
  d_{\mathrm{act}}(\gW) \;=\; \dim\nolimits_{\varepsilon} \gM_{\gW} .
\end{equation}

Here \emph{$\varepsilon$ stays finite}, whereas a box-counting dimension is ordinarily defined as a
limit $\varepsilon \to 0$. It must stay finite because the estimator reaches $\gM_{\gW}$ only
through distances between nearby samples. \emph{The window must also be long enough}: any path
resembles a curve over a short stretch, giving $d_{\mathrm{act}} = 1$ unless $\gW$ outlasts the return
time of the trajectory.

The systems of \cref{sec:ladder} set $\vc_t = F(\varphi_1(t), \dots, \varphi_r(t))$, with
$\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_r$ independent quasiperiodic phases. Because the phases are rationally independent, the path lies on an
$r$-dimensional torus and passes arbitrarily close to every point of it, so $d_{\mathrm{act}} = r$ at every $\varepsilon$ below the amplitude of the
weakest phase. Above that scale the weakest phases are invisible and $d_{\mathrm{act}}$ declines
with the resolution, as the definition requires (\cref{sec:aniso}).

We also measure the participation ratio \citep{gao2017theory}, which for a non-negative spectrum
$\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pr}
  \PR(\lambda) \;=\; \frac{\bigl(\sum_i \lambda_i\bigr)^2}{\sum_i \lambda_i^2} .
\end{equation}
It equals $m$ exactly when $m$ of the $\lambda_i$ are equal and the rest vanish. Applied to the
eigenvalues of a covariance over the window it gives the \emph{effective rank}, in two forms named
by the superscript:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prvar}
  \PR^{\mathrm{pos}} = \PR\bigl(\Cov \vc_t\bigr), \qquad
  \PR^{\mathrm{det}} = \PR\bigl(\Cov \tilde{\vc}_t\bigr),
\end{equation}
both over $t \in \gW$, with $\tilde{\vc}_t$ the residual of $\vc_t$ about its per-window
least-squares line. A drifting trajectory needs the detrended
form, since a straight drift dominates the spectrum and holds $\PR^{\mathrm{pos}}$ near unity
however the trajectory moves about that line. Written without a superscript, \emph{the effective
rank} means $\PR^{\mathrm{pos}}$.

The effective rank is not the active dimension. It equals $r$ when $r$ directions carry comparable
variance and falls below $r$ when they do not, while $d_{\mathrm{act}}$ stays at $r$
(\cref{sec:aniso}). We use it to check that each system of \cref{sec:ladder} really does excite $r$
independent phases (\cref{tab:gt}). It is also the quantity \cref{sec:direct} measures on a stored
grokking trajectory, in the setting where a log yields no dimension at all.

\subsection{Observation model and estimator}\label{sec:estimator}

We observe a scalar series $x_t = \phi(\vs_t)$, in which $\vs_t$ is the full optimiser state and
$\phi$ is fixed before the run. From it we reconstruct
$\vy_t = (x_t, \dots, x_{t-(E-1)\tau}) \in \R^E$. For a
generic pair of dynamics and observation on a compact invariant set of box-counting dimension $d$,
this map is an embedding once $E > 2d$ (\cref{thm:takens,thm:sauer}). Genericity is not verifiable
for a $\phi$ fixed in advance, so every result below is reported over a family of observers. We
estimate the intrinsic dimension of $\{\vy_t\}$ by maximum likelihood on nearest-neighbour
distances (\cref{thm:lb}). Let
$r^{(i)}_1 \le \dots \le r^{(i)}_m$ be the distances from point $i$ to its $m$ nearest neighbours,
where a neighbour counts only if it is more than $W_T$ steps away from $i$ in time. Discarding the
near-in-time neighbours in this way is the \emph{Theiler exclusion} \citep{theiler1986spurious,
kantz2004nonlinear}: without it, the nearest points in $\R^E$ are the samples just before and after
$i$, so the statistic measures the sampling rate instead of the geometry. Pooling the likelihood over the $N$ points before inverting, as
\citet{mackay2005comments} recommend, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mg}
  \hat{d}_{\mathrm{MG}}^{\,-1} \;=\; \frac{1}{N(m-1)} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{m-1}
  \log \frac{r^{(i)}_m}{r^{(i)}_j} ,
\end{equation}
while averaging the per-point estimates instead, as \citet{levina2004maximum} propose, gives the
variant we write LB. We write \emph{the estimate} for the value MG returns and \emph{the estimator}
for the procedure; \cref{tab:alts} reports both and \cref{app:alg} gives the algorithm as
implemented.
The \emph{roughness} $\operatorname{std}(\Delta x)/\operatorname{std}(x)$, the \emph{linear
participation ratio} $\PR_{\mathrm{delay}}$ of \cref{eq:pr} on $\Cov\{\vy_t\}$ and the
\emph{spectral participation ratio} on the power spectrum of $x$ accompany every estimate.
Each can grow with $r$ for reasons that involve no geometry. If one of them recovers the truth as
well as the estimator does, the accuracy of the estimator is no evidence that a dimension has been
measured.

\subsection{Dynamical regimes}\label{sec:classes}

Two conditions must hold before an estimate computed from a log may be read as an active
dimension. The embedding theorems (\cref{thm:takens,thm:sauer}) require a deterministic map on a
compact invariant set; that licenses the delay reconstruction as a change of coordinates. The
neighbour statistic of \cref{eq:mg} requires more: the record must cover that set densely enough
that a point's nearest neighbours in $\R^E$ are returns to its neighbourhood rather than the
samples immediately before and after it. The Theiler exclusion enforces this and
\cref{sec:diagnostics} tests it. The estimator itself would accept points drawn from independent
runs; recurrence is needed because our entire sample is one trajectory. An optimiser near a
solution may be in any of three regimes. Only the first satisfies both conditions.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt, parsep=0pt]
\item \textbf{Deterministic and recurrent.} The path returns close to states already passed through
and lies on an $r$-dimensional torus, so the occupied set has $r$ independent components and
$d_{\mathrm{act}} = r$. Here both conditions hold and the estimator recovers $r$. This is the only
regime in which it does.

\item \textbf{Deterministic and transient.} A converging run decays along a curve it never
revisits, so the occupied set is that curve and $d_{\mathrm{act}} = 1$. The estimand exists, but the
protocol misses it. The record contains no returns: in place of $1$ the estimator reports about
$29$ at every $r$, a value produced by the Theiler exclusion itself (\cref{sec:regime}). The
effective rank does stay near unity here (\cref{tab:gt}), but only because the curve is nearly
straight.

\item \textbf{Stochastically driven.} Mini-batch gradient noise leaves no low-dimensional invariant
set for the path to fill, so there is no active dimension to estimate; the value returned is set by
$E_{\max}$ and by the record length. The embedding theorems for forced systems (\cref{thm:stark})
do cover a drive of this kind, but one realisation of it at a time, whereas a single run crosses
realisations.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Diagnostics}\label{sec:diagnostics}

The series alone is enough to tell a transient from a stochastic drive. The \textbf{identifiability
ratio} $\rho_{\mathrm{ident}} = \hat{d}_{\mathrm{MG}}(2E_{\max}) / \hat{d}_{\mathrm{MG}}(E_{\max})$
compares the estimate at a doubled embedding dimension with the estimate at the working one. It
stays near unity where a dimension can be measured. Under mini-batch noise the estimate follows the
embedding dimension rather than the data; the ratio then grows. A transient escapes
it: there the estimate is stable in the embedding dimension for reasons unconnected with any
dimension. A transient is caught by the \textbf{trend-crossing count} instead, the number of sign
changes of the residual of the window about its own least-squares line, which is near zero on a
monotone decay. The count tests only non-monotonicity, so it cannot replace the ratio; we report
both with every estimate. \Cref{sec:diagnostics-validated} validates both.

\section{Validation protocol}\label{sec:protocol}

Four requirements were fixed before any result was computed. \Cref{tab:ladder} records which of
them each system fails.

\emph{Constructed truth}: the ground truth is measured rather than assumed. Every system is built
with $r$ independent quasiperiodic phases, so that $d_{\mathrm{act}} = r$ by construction. The
effective rank of the recorded trajectory must then confirm that all $r$ are comparably excited.

\emph{Frozen configuration}: the estimator is chosen once, by a search over its own settings alone.
Searching the settings of the system at the same time would select the data together with the
method: the result would then report how favourable a system the search had found.

\emph{Withheld ranks}: ranks are held out as well as seeds, because the geometry of the excitation
depends on $r$ and every seed at a given rank shares that geometry.

\emph{Zero learning rate}: every system is re-run with the learning rate set to zero, so that the
parameters never move and any observer of the optimiser must be constant. A log that still varies
and still returns its earlier estimate was never a function of the optimiser. The frozen nonlinear
decoder and the perceptron in a $k$-subspace fail it.

\section{Evaluation on systems with a known active dimension}\label{sec:ladder}

We evaluate the estimator on six systems of increasing realism: an oscillating diagonal matrix,
online linear regression, logistic regression, a frozen nonlinear decoder, a perceptron confined to
a $k$-dimensional subspace, and a linear head on a network trained on image data. In each, the
path lies on an $r$-dimensional torus by construction, giving
$d_{\mathrm{act}} = r$; the measured effective rank confirms that all $r$ phases are excited;
the estimator receives one scalar series and nothing else. \Cref{tab:ladder} collects the outcome;
the simplest and the most realistic are described below.

\subsection{An oscillating diagonal matrix}\label{sec:matrix}

The first system has no optimiser and no feedback. In $\mW(t) = \operatorname{diag}(b_i +
\delta_i(t))$, the offsets $b_i$ are fixed and $r$ of the $\delta_i$ oscillate at rationally
independent frequencies. The estimate tracks $r$ across the whole withheld range to within a third
of a component (\cref{tab:ladder}).

\subsection{A constrained head on a network trained on image data}\label{sec:digits}

A tanh network is trained on the scikit-learn handwritten digits and then frozen. A linear head
confined to a $k$-dimensional affine subspace is trained on top of it under softmax
cross-entropy, so that the curvature is data-dependent and all the dynamics occur in $\vc$. The
excitation is made $r$-dimensional by modulating the loss weights of $r$ of twelve fixed data
groups and orthogonalising the resulting gradient directions (\cref{app:constructions}). We drive
the head quasiperiodically, with mini-batch noise, and by plain gradient descent, covering the
recurrent, stochastic and transient regimes of \cref{sec:classes}.

Twelve scalar observers are recorded (\cref{app:obs}). The estimator was frozen on four of them,
on seeds and ranks reserved for that purpose. Every result below is therefore out of sample in
rank, in seed, and for most observers in the observer too. In the deterministic recurrent regime the
estimate recovers the active dimension to within about one component, though the error differs by a
factor of five between the best observer and the worst (\cref{tab:obs}). Every observer orders the
withheld ranks correctly (\cref{tab:ladder}). For none of the ten scored observers does the
roughness of the log order those ranks, so the agreement cannot be explained by the logs merely
becoming rougher as $r$ grows. At $k = 20$ the same system behaves alike at a lower ceiling
(\cref{tab:k20}). We
mark that result exploratory, because it was scored with a Theiler exclusion smaller than the
protocol requires (\cref{app:theiler}).

\paragraph{The ceiling.}\label{sec:ceiling} The estimator saturates above about eight components.
The embedding dimension sets the level it returns at high $r$, while the largest $r$ it still
tracks follows neither the embedding dimension nor the record length and never exceeds about
eleven (\cref{app:ceiling}). One caveat is substantial. The participation ratio of the delay
covariance, a purely linear statistic, saturates along the same curve, so the limit may be the
number of components the delay window resolves linearly, with no geometry involved.

\paragraph{The zero-learning-rate test.}\label{sec:silence} The zero-learning-rate check requires
that an observer of a frozen optimiser be constant. Most published analyses of dimension on
training logs do not run it. It invalidated two of our systems, which reproduce their reported
results with the parameters frozen. It also rejects the instantaneous mini-batch loss of
\cref{sec:digits}, otherwise among the more accurate observers.

\subsection{Recovery in each regime}\label{sec:regime}

\Cref{fig:regimes} evaluates the estimator in each of the three regimes of \cref{sec:classes}. Its
two panels ask the same question with and without the ground truth. Panel (a) shows which cases the
estimator recovers correctly. Panel (b) gives the conclusion a user with no ground truth to check
against would have drawn about the same cases.

In (a) the fast quasiperiodic drive follows the diagonal over the whole withheld range. Under
mini-batch noise the estimate is flat in $r$ and well above the truth, responding to $E_{\max}$ and
to the length of the record instead of to the data. Weak noise on an otherwise recurrent trajectory
leaves the ordering intact and destroys the absolute value. The slow drive is the informative case,
because it changes behaviour partway along: it tracks the truth to about $r = 4$, then saturates.

Panel (b) gives the identifiability ratio for the same cases at $r = 2$ and $r = 6$, on either side
of that change. The regime labels alone would have predicted the extreme cases, since the fast drive
passes at both ranks and the transient is furthest from passing at both. They would not have
predicted the slow drive, which passes at $r = 2$, where panel (a) puts the estimate on the
diagonal, and fails at $r = 6$, where panel (a) puts it at little more than half the truth. The
ratio therefore locates the failure inside a single system, using nothing the ground truth
supplied.

The Theiler exclusion is the reason the estimator errs most on a transient. With the exclusion
removed, it returns at every rank, seed and observer the value maximum likelihood gives on a
uniformly sampled curve, while the fast recurrent case is unchanged (\cref{app:exclusion}). The value
near $29$ is a divergent quantity evaluated at the cap on the exclusion; we leave it unclamped
(\cref{app:alg}).

Every system evaluated above is externally driven, whereas training is not, so the evaluation
establishes only a conditional result. We therefore ask whether the full-batch runs of
\cref{sec:grokking} reach the edge of stability \citep{cohen2021gradient}, where an undriven run
might still recur. They do reach it, but oscillation is not
recurrence. Some of the runs at the edge never return close to a state already passed through;
the diagnostics reject them all alike (\cref{app:eos}).

\subsection{The delay lag}\label{sec:tau}

The delay window spans $(E_{\max}-1)\tau$ samples. It must cover an appreciable fraction of the
oscillation period. Below that the delay coordinates are nearly collinear and the torus never
unfolds \citep{casdagli1991state}. Varying $\tau$ alone on a fixed torus moves the estimate over
more than an order of magnitude, through the truth only where the window covers a few tenths of a
period (\cref{fig:tau}). The lag must therefore be set from the
oscillation period. A constructed system supplies that period and a training log does not. Since we
cannot recover it from the log, \cref{sec:grokking} reads changes and never a level.

\subsection{Nuisance factors}\label{sec:nuisance}

At $r = 4$ we vary factors that leave $r$ unchanged over five seeds and twelve observers,
measuring each shift against the shift a real change of four components produces
(\cref{tab:controls}). The shifts worth reporting come from the ramped observer gain and the ramped
state amplitude. Smoothness is not among them: shifting the drive band by an octave changes
the roughness sharply while moving the estimate by well under a fifth of that reference shift, on
every observer. A ramped gain moves the estimate by about a component, so a run whose parameter
norm grows steadily will drift by that much at fixed $r$. This is the confound that matters most in
\cref{sec:grokking}.

Roughness tracks the estimate closely here and again in \cref{sec:matched}, closely enough to
raise a prior question: whether the estimate and the roughness can be separated at all.
\Cref{app:clocks} separates them both ways, holding one fixed while the other moves.

\subsection{The estimand is a dimension, not an effective rank}\label{sec:aniso}

Every construction in \cref{sec:ladder} drives its $r$ phases at equal amplitude, which makes the
active dimension and the effective rank of \cref{sec:three} numerically equal. Where two candidate
estimands agree, an experiment cannot say which of them the estimator reports. Driving phase $l$ at
amplitude $q^{l}$ separates them: the path still lies on an $r$-dimensional torus, while the
effective rank falls as $q$ falls. The estimate follows the active dimension (\cref{fig:aniso}),
staying closer to $r$ than to the effective rank and registering only the phases
it can resolve.

\paragraph{The diagnostics.}\label{sec:diagnostics-validated} Across this grid the identifiability
ratio again separates the accurate cases from the inaccurate ones without overlap, now within a
single regime as well as between regimes. The ratio and the trend-crossing count are calibrated on
these cases alone, so the band drawn in \cref{sec:grokking} records where the accurate cases fell
and carries no authority beyond them.

\section{Application to grokking}\label{sec:grokking}

We use two grokking settings. A transformer is trained with weight decay and AdamW on
mini-batches, which places it in the stochastic regime of \cref{sec:classes}; a two-layer perceptron
is trained by unregularised full-batch gradient descent (\cref{sec:related}; \cref{app:runs} lists
every run). In both, the diagnostics reject the level the logs report, so we store the trajectory
and measure it directly, then return to the logs to ask whether a change within one run can still
be read.

\subsection{The estimate may not be read as a dimension in either setting}\label{sec:grok-diagnostics}

We run the frozen configuration of \cref{sec:digits} on these logs, shortening the window and
stride to fit the records and changing nothing else (\cref{tab:grok-diagnostics}, \cref{fig:map}).
The transformer runs and the perceptron runs are rejected for different reasons. In neither case
is the reason the regime label we would have assigned in advance. The identifiability ratio of
the transformer runs lies in a band the deterministic regimes never reach. Their estimates also
scatter widely across seeds of one configuration, exactly as \cref{fig:regimes} shows mini-batch
noise behaving. The perceptron runs carry the signature of a transient instead: two trend crossings in a whole
record, at a lower ratio that is still far outside the band. The value may therefore be read as a dimension in
neither setting. A change within one run might still be read, since a fixed configuration adds the
same offset to every window of that run, but not at this window, which spans tens of thousands of
optimiser steps against a transition a few hundred steps wide. The window is shortened in
\cref{sec:matched} (\cref{app:window}).

\paragraph{Label-matched pairs.}\label{sec:pairs} The perceptron targets come in pairs differing by
a single monomial, both members of which fit the training set completely under identical
optimisation. Since the members of a pair remain different polynomials, a separation between them
could still be a separation between tasks. The estimate does separate them, but we rest nothing on
it. Every one of these runs is transient; the raw loss curves already show the separation
(\cref{app:pairs,app:fb,app:falls}).

\subsection{The trajectory simplifies at generalisation}\label{sec:direct}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_dip.pdf}
\caption{Simplification of the trajectory at generalisation. Three regularised seeds of modular
addition and one $S_5$ composition, against the same two configurations trained without weight
decay, aligned on the generalisation step of the run each matches. Thick line, the mean of the four
generalising runs; band, the spread between the two hash families (\cref{app:sketch}). Panels (a)
and (b) are the one run whose training curve is stored. Panels (c)--(e) plot $\PR^{\mathrm{det}}$
and the displacement over windows of $600$ steps, $300$ for $S_5$; (f) the estimate of
\cref{sec:matched} on the log of (b).}
\label{fig:dip}
\end{figure}

The effective rank of \cref{sec:three} is defined whether or not the path returns on itself, so a
stored trajectory allows a measurement that a log does not. We store the trajectory of a
transformer trained to grok, compressed at every logged step by a random projection of the
parameter vector and of the probe logits (\cref{app:sketch}). Near generalisation the effective
rank collapses in both spaces, reaching near unity in parameter space. In each space the minimum
falls within a few hundred steps of the transition. Both then re-expand (\cref{fig:dip},
\cref{tab:dip}). Two competing readings can be set aside. The collapse might follow training
progress, but it occurs at generalisation in seeds whose generalisation steps differ several-fold
and in a second task. It might record a trajectory that has come to a halt, but the total
displacement reaches its floor thousands of steps later and never recovers, whereas the effective
rank does recover. One caveat stands. The effective rank measures variation inside the window.
Only that variation collapses; $d_{\mathrm{act}}$ itself may be unchanged.

\paragraph{The runs without weight decay.}\label{sec:nowd} The same two configurations trained
without weight decay memorise and stay at chance accuracy. Within the transition window they
decline much less than the generalising runs, but somewhere in training one of them declines just
as far (\cref{tab:dip}). Depth therefore does not separate the generalising runs from the
memorising ones; the timing of the decline separates them, as does whether it reverses. A low effective rank is reached both by a run that has come
to rest and by one about to generalise.

\subsection{The log estimate falls where the trajectory does}\label{sec:matched}

The test \cref{sec:grok-diagnostics} could not perform becomes possible once the window is set in
units of the transition, not as a fraction of the record. We re-run the estimator on the
same logs, at the window length and midpoints the direct measurement uses, so that both statistics
are sampled at the same instants. The frozen configuration does not fit a window that short, while
the frozen-configuration requirement forbids choosing a replacement by how well it performs. We
therefore report every cell of a grid, taking as headline the cell named by a rule blind to the
outcome (\cref{app:window}).

On the parameter norm the estimate halves at the generalisation step in all four generalising runs,
its minimum falling on the same side of the transition as the minimum of the direct measurement
(\cref{fig:dip}f). That drop is among the largest any of the four ever shows, while the other two runs show nothing
remarkable there; all but two of the usable grid cells separate the generalising runs from the
other two (\cref{tab:matched}). The drop
also survives a check on the shape of the log. We compare each run
against randomised series built from it that keep its slow shape and its fluctuation spectrum while
destroying its fine structure. The fraction of those series falling as steeply as the run itself is
smaller for every generalising run than for either of the other two (\cref{app:window}).

The same event is thus recorded twice: once by storing the trajectory, once in a log a run writes
anyway. The second record is the weaker. Its level cannot be read as a dimension: only the change
within a run is used. Its observer is the parameter norm, for which \cref{sec:nuisance}
leaves a smoothness explanation open, so the comparison against randomised series carries the claim
alone. The roughness of these windows also collapses by an order of magnitude where the estimate
merely halves; a simpler statistic registers the change more strongly.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}

In a deterministic recurrent system with a matched delay lag, one scalar log of a suitable observer
counts the independent components of the set the parameters occupy, to within about one component
and up to about eight. Outside that regime it does not: a transient supplies no returns;
mini-batch noise leaves no invariant set to measure. The estimator is usable only alongside the
identifiability ratio and the trend-crossing count, which place both grokking settings outside the
regime it needs. Our result there comes from the stored trajectory, whose effective rank collapses
at generalisation and then re-expands.
\paragraph{Limitations.} The measurement needs a recurrent regime and a lag matched to a period no
log reveals. Above about eight components it is only ordinal. The collapse comes from four runs
against two, distinguished by weight decay, which also drives the parameter norm; it marks the
transition only once the transition has happened. It does not reproduce in the full-batch
setting. The drop at the matched window is close to the drop a ramped gain alone produces. The
edge-of-stability evidence comes from one task (\cref{app:config,app:fb,app:window,app:eos}).

\section{The results behind the estimator}\label{app:theory}

The procedure of \cref{sec:estimator} is assembled from four published results, stated here without
proof and in the form the paper uses. Every one of the conditions they impose is an assumption
about the run rather than a property we can verify from the log.

Throughout, $f$ is the map advancing the system by one step, $\phi$ the scalar observation, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delaymap}
  \Phi_{f,\phi}(\vs) \;=\; \bigl(\phi(\vs),\, \phi(f(\vs)),\, \dots,\, \phi(f^{E-1}(\vs))\bigr)
  \;\in\; \R^{E}
\end{equation}
the delay map.

\begin{theorem}[Delay embedding; \citealp{takens1981detecting}]\label{thm:takens}
Let $M$ be a compact manifold of dimension $d$ and let $E > 2d$. For a generic pair $(f, \phi)$
with $f \in C^2(M, M)$ a diffeomorphism and $\phi \in C^2(M, \R)$, the delay map
\cref{eq:delaymap} is an embedding of $M$ into $\R^{E}$.
\end{theorem}

An embedding is one-to-one and an immersion, so distances in $\R^E$ between reconstructed points
are a faithful, though distorted, image of distances between states. The set they fill is the
object the neighbour statistic below measures (\cref{fig:shapes}). \Cref{thm:takens} requires a
manifold.

The sets a training run occupies need not be manifolds; the extension below is the version the
paper relies on.
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_shapes.pdf}
\caption{The set the neighbour search measures, in the plane of the first two delay coordinates.
One window of the image-data system of \cref{sec:digits} under each excitation, reconstructed at
the frozen configuration. A single phase closes an orbit, two fill a surface, a converging run
never returns, and mini-batch noise fills the space it is given.}
\label{fig:shapes}
\end{figure}

\begin{theorem}[Fractal delay embedding prevalence; \citealp{sauer1991embedology}]\label{thm:sauer}
Let $A \subset \R^{n}$ be compact with box-counting dimension $d$, invariant under a smooth
diffeomorphism $f$ defined on an open neighbourhood of $A$, and let $E > 2d$ be an integer. Assume
that for each $p \le E$ the set of periodic points of $f$ of period $p$ has box-counting dimension
less than $p/2$, and that the linearisation of $f$ at each such orbit has distinct eigenvalues.
Then for almost every smooth $\phi$, in the sense of prevalence, the delay map \cref{eq:delaymap}
is one-to-one on $A$ and an immersion on every compact subset of a smooth manifold contained
in $A$.
\end{theorem}

The dimension in the condition $E > 2d$ is the box-counting dimension of \cref{eq:act}, which need
not be an integer, so the criterion applies to the sets a training run occupies and not only to
tori. Prevalence, in turn, is a measure-theoretic notion of ``almost every'' on an
infinite-dimensional space.

Mini-batch sampling makes the update depend on a draw from the data as well as on the state, so
neither theorem above applies as stated.

\begin{theorem}[Delay embedding for forced systems;
\citealp{stark1999delay, stark2003delay}]\label{thm:stark}
Let $\vs_{t+1} = f(\vs_t, w_t)$ with the drive $w_t$ generated by a measure-preserving system on a
space $\Omega$, either deterministically or as an independent draw selecting among finitely many
maps. Under genericity conditions on $f$ and $\phi$ analogous to those of \cref{thm:takens}, and
for $E$ exceeding twice the dimension of a fibre, the map sending a state to its delay vector
\cref{eq:delaymap} is an embedding \emph{of each fibre} $M_{\omega}$, that is, of the set of states
compatible with one realisation $\omega$ of the drive.
\end{theorem}

The guarantee holds fibre by fibre, whereas the sample available in practice is one trajectory,
which lies in a different fibre at every step. No invariant measure on a fibre is being sampled, so
the neighbour statistic below estimates nothing.

\begin{theorem}[Maximum likelihood intrinsic dimension;
\citealp{levina2004maximum, mackay2005comments}]\label{thm:lb}
Let points be drawn from a smooth density supported on a $d$-dimensional manifold embedded in
$\R^{E}$. Model the points falling within a small ball about $\vy_i$ as a homogeneous Poisson
process in the $d$-dimensional radial variable, with the density constant over the ball. Writing
$r^{(i)}_j$ for the distance from $\vy_i$ to its $j$-th nearest neighbour, the maximum likelihood
estimate of $d$ from the first $m$ neighbours of that point is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lbpoint}
  \hat{d}_m(\vy_i)^{-1} \;=\; \frac{1}{m-1} \sum_{j=1}^{m-1} \log \frac{r^{(i)}_m}{r^{(i)}_j} .
\end{equation}
Under this model the reciprocal is unbiased and the estimate itself is not.
\end{theorem}

The estimator is therefore a local statistic: it reports the dimension at the scale set by the $m$
nearest neighbours, the finite $\varepsilon$ of \cref{eq:act} rather than a limit as
$\varepsilon \to 0$. A record of $N$ points resolves a dimension of at most about
$2 \log_{10} N$ under the argument of \citet{eckmann1992fundamental}. A series with a power-law
spectrum returns a finite, reproducible and entirely spurious value fixed by its spectral exponent
\citep{osborne1989finite, theiler1991some}. Neither bound explains the ceiling we measure, which
\cref{app:ceiling} takes up.

\section{The estimator, its configuration and the observers}\label{app:method}

\subsection{The estimator}\label{app:alg}

\Cref{alg:mg} is the estimator as it was run. It computes \cref{eq:mg} with three departures that
affect the values reported.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Pooled Levina--Bickel intrinsic dimension of a delay reconstruction}
\label{alg:mg}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require series $x_{1:n}$; lag $\tau$; embedding dimension $E$; neighbours $m$; Theiler
exclusion $W_T$; dither $\sigma$
\State $z_t \gets (x_t - \operatorname{mean} x) / \operatorname{std} x + \sigma \epsilon_t$,\quad
       $\epsilon_t \sim \gN(0,1)$ \Comment{breaks exact ties}
\State $\vy_t \gets (z_t, z_{t-\tau}, \dots, z_{t-(E-1)\tau})$ for $t = (E-1)\tau+1, \dots, n$
\State $S \gets 0$; $N \gets 0$; $n_{\mathrm{dist}} \gets 0$; $n_{\mathrm{ratio}} \gets 0$
\For{each $\vy_i$}
  \State $r^{(i)}_1 \le \dots \le r^{(i)}_m \gets$ distances to the $m$ nearest $\vy_j$
         with $|i-j| > W_T$
  \State $r^{(i)}_j \gets \max(r^{(i)}_j, \varepsilon_{\mathrm{dist}})$ for each $j$; count those
         clipped into $n_{\mathrm{dist}}$
  \State $S_i \gets \max\bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{m-1} \log(r^{(i)}_m / r^{(i)}_j),\,
         \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ratio}}\bigr)$; count clips into $n_{\mathrm{ratio}}$
  \State $S \gets S + S_i$; \quad $N \gets N + 1$
\EndFor
\State mark the window degenerate if $n_{\mathrm{dist}}$ or $n_{\mathrm{ratio}}$ exceeds
       $1\,\%$ of its denominator
\State \Return $\bigl(N(m-1) - 1\bigr) / S$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\emph{The returned value is $(N(m-1)-1)/S$}, the exactly unbiased pooled form, which differs from
\cref{eq:mg} by less than $10^{-5}$ at the sizes used here. \emph{A window marked degenerate is
returned rather than discarded.} No published cell of \cref{tab:grok-diagnostics} contains a marked
window. \emph{The returned value is never clamped to $E$}, so a divergent estimate is reported as
divergent.

The Theiler exclusion is $W_T = \max\bigl((E-1)\tau,\, t_{\mathrm{acf}}\bigr)$, where
$t_{\mathrm{acf}}$ is the first lag at which the autocorrelation falls below $1/e$, capped at $150$
samples because the neighbour query cost grows linearly with it; \cref{app:theiler} measures the
effect of that cap. The floors $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dist}}$, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ratio}}$ and
the dither $\sigma$ are $10^{-8}$, $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-9}$.

\section{System constructions}\label{app:constructions}

\paragraph{The image-data system of \cref{sec:digits}.} The data is partitioned into twelve fixed
groups, the same partition at every rank so that the partition is not itself a function of $r$. The
loss weights of $r$ of those groups are modulated by $r$ rationally independent sinusoids filling
one octave. Modulating group $j$ tilts the gradient along a direction $\vphi_j$. These
directions are neither orthogonal nor of equal effect, since random data groups have correlated
gradients, so a mixing matrix $\mathrm{pinv}(\Phi) Q$ with $Q$ an orthonormal basis of
$\operatorname{range}(\Phi)$ orthogonalises them. The per-phase scalar response gain of the
linearised update is then divided out. Without this correction the trajectory participation ratio
falls well below $r$.

\Cref{tab:gt} verifies that each construction excites as many independent directions as it is meant
to. Two of its rows report an outcome rather than a setting. A decaying transient does not recur
whatever $r$ is, since a converging trajectory is a curve. Plain mini-batch descent holds the same
rank regardless of $r$, because the rank of its noise is fixed by the per-example gradient
covariance of the data; projecting that noise onto $r$ directions recovers only part of the
intended rank, so the estimates in that regime are scored against the measured value.

coordinate and the student tracks it by exact backpropagation through both layers. The
functional, trajectory-covariance and update-covariance ranks were all verified to equal $r$.

it, and the decaying transient stays near unity throughout.

\section{The direct trajectory measurement}\label{app:direct}

\subsection{The trajectory sketch}\label{app:sketch}

The direct measurement of \cref{sec:direct} needs the trajectory itself: the whole parameter vector
at each of two to three thousand logged steps. Every logged step is therefore
compressed to $\R^{1024}$ by a CountSketch \citep{charikar2002finding, weinberger2009feature}, a
sparse random projection, drawn twice from two independent hash families so that the two copies can
be compared. Two spaces are recorded: the parameters, and the probe logits after centring each
example over classes and normalising to unit length. The normalisation is necessary because the raw
logit scale is mechanically coupled to weight decay; without it, a signal taken from the logits
could not be distinguished from a measurement of weight decay.

We do not verify a spectral guarantee for the compression. The two hash families supply a direct
estimate of the error instead: every reported value is the mean of the two projections, with the
disagreement between them recorded beside it. That disagreement is a per cent of the value at the
median and under nine per cent at its worst, smaller than every effect \cref{sec:direct}
reports.

\emph{The sketch is accurate on synthetic trajectories of known rank.} \Cref{tab:sketch} passes
such trajectories through the sketch and compares the result against the same statistic computed on
the uncompressed trajectory. The sketch is accurate to $0.11$ components throughout. Nothing above
rank ten is tested, however, whereas the plateaus in \cref{fig:dip} are higher.

\emph{It agrees with exact arithmetic on a real run.} On the perceptron architecture, where the
parameter count is small enough to allow it, the participation ratio computed on all $145\,500$ raw
parameters agrees with the sketched value to three decimals.

\emph{Recording the trajectory does not perturb training.} The log is required to be bit-identical
with and without the recording, over four hundred steps at reduced probe size. That check matters
because a single global random stream supplies the train--validation split, the initialisation and
the mini-batch order in turn, so one stray draw changes the initial weights and can destroy
grokking.

\section{The resolution of a windowed statistic}\label{app:resolution}

\subsection{The full-batch setting}\label{app:fb}

Repeating the direct measurement on the perceptron setting gives a result that must be read as a
limit of the measurement rather than as a negative finding. Under full-batch gradient descent every
window of every run has a participation ratio between $1.000$ and $1.362$ on the statistics the
comparison uses.

A deterministic update makes the trajectory a smooth curve. Over a $600$-step window such a curve
is straight to within the measurement precision, so the statistic reports the curvature of the
trajectory at the scale the window sets, where there is almost none. The statistic has
no range in which a collapse could appear. Its absence is therefore not evidence that no collapse
occurred.

Repeating the measurement at a range of window lengths settles the question. Two runs of $150\,000$ steps, a generalising one and its label-matched
partner, are measured over windows spanning $600$ to $118\,000$ optimiser steps. Each window holds
the same sixty samples, spread further apart as the window lengthens rather than supplemented by
new ones, so that the bound on the statistic and its noise floor stay exactly where the published
measurement put them. \Cref{tab:prwindow} gives the result. The statistic does leave its floor, at a
scale of about $10^4$ steps, more than an order of magnitude above the published window. The absence of a feature at the shorter window was
therefore a limit of resolution rather than a fact about the run.

The feature that appears at the longer window is not a signal that was previously concealed. One
localised maximum appears, of the same size in both runs (\cref{tab:prwindow}). Function space
agrees, with more range and no more separation. Nor can that maximum be placed relative to
memorisation or to generalisation: a window this long cannot place a feature to better than the
interval between memorisation and generalisation.

At the window where the statistic has range, it does not separate the generalising run from its label-matched control. Beyond $30\,000$

\cref{sec:direct} belongs to the regularised stochastic setting.

\subsection{Declines of the estimate that are not generalisation}\label{app:falls}

A decline of the estimate is not specific to generalisation. In a long run at $p = 211$ with no
weight decay, which memorises early and ends at chance validation accuracy, a decline is detected
tens of thousands of steps after memorisation. The ramped-gain experiment of \cref{sec:nuisance}
supplies a mechanism for it that needs no dimension at all: a ramped observer gain moves the
estimate by about a component, while the parameter norm of this run roughly triples and acts on
the estimate through the observer.

The trajectory of that run does not simplify. Its diagnostics place it in the transient regime
(\cref{fig:map}), where the estimate is not a dimension. The direct measurement of \cref{sec:direct} is an effective rank of the
trajectory itself; it declines in four generalising runs and in neither of the two without weight
decay. The estimate on a log, at the frozen window and in these settings, is neither a dimension
nor an effective rank; it declines in at least one run that does not generalise.

\Cref{sec:matched} does not make the decline specific. The estimate on a log does decline where the
trajectory does, in four runs and in neither of the other two, but at a window two orders of
magnitude shorter than the one used here, under a configuration chosen for that window. Nor is a
run's failure to generalise within the allotted steps evidence that a decline in it was a false
alarm.

\subsection{Resolution of a windowed estimate on a training log}\label{app:window}

A change within one run might still be read where the level may not be. That possibility is
testable on the seven extended runs. The frozen configuration takes a window of a third of each
record, $39\,990$ optimiser steps, advanced by $10\,000$ steps at a time. That gives nine windows
per run, localising a feature only to $\pm 19\,995$ steps.

The runs therefore cannot be aligned on their generalisation steps as \cref{fig:dip} aligns them.
No two runs share an offset, so no mean over runs can be formed.

The one run with a window centre near its generalisation step falls by $3.54$ components over the
following stride, more than twice the $1.53$-component floor that a ramped gain alone produces
(\cref{sec:nuisance}). The windows in which the estimate
falls are the windows in which the parameter norm is itself moving fastest, so the fall is the
very ramped gain the floor is built from. The other two runs show nothing.

Nor is any of this specific to generalisation. Across the fifty-six stride-to-stride changes
(\cref{fig:window}), six
of twenty-four exceed that floor in the runs that generalise and six of thirty-two in the runs that
do not. The largest change in the set, $6.36$ components, is in a run that does generalise, but it
occurs nearly ninety thousand steps after its generalisation step.

As in \cref{app:fb}, the statistic has no range in which a feature confined to the transition could
appear. Testing the claim requires a window set in units of the width of the transition, which for
these runs is hundreds of steps. That means re-running the estimator, not re-reading the existing
records.

\paragraph{The matched-window re-run.} \Cref{sec:matched} performs that re-run on the six runs of
\cref{fig:dip} rather than the seven of this appendix: the question needs a paired comparison and
only those six had their trajectory stored. Each window is $60$ samples, centred on the midpoint of
a window of \cref{sec:direct}.

The depth of the fall is measured by $D$, the median estimate over the three thousand steps before
the transition divided by the minimum over the window that brackets it. Its significance is
assessed against the surrogates of \cref{sec:matched} \citep{theiler1992testing,
schreiber1996improved}. \Cref{tab:matched} gives both.

\emph{The frozen configuration does not fit.} At $60$ samples the frozen delay span of $76$ exceeds
the window, so some other configuration has to be used. The grid crosses $E_{\max} \in \{4, 6, 10\}$,
$\tau \in \{1, 2, 4\}$, $m \in \{5, 20\}$ and both Theiler rules, giving thirty-six cells, of which
twenty-six are long enough to return a value. Twenty-four of those separate the four generalising
runs from the other two. The headline cell is named by the rule of \cref{sec:matched}, which does
not choose the best cell: a neighbouring cell separates the runs more sharply.

\emph{The diagnostics do not survive the shortening.} The identifiability ratio needs an embedding
of $2E_{\max}$, so at the headline cell the Theiler exclusion leaves too few usable points to
compute one. Where the ratio can be computed at all, the two Theiler rules disagree about whether
it passes; it settles nothing.

\emph{Linear detrending removes the effect.} Repeating every cell on
a linearly detrended window, the removal $\PR^{\mathrm{det}}$ performs coordinate by coordinate,
leaves no cell that separates the generalising runs from the rest. Detrending a sketch and
detrending a scalar are not comparable: on a $1024$-dimensional sketch a per-coordinate linear
detrend removes one direction out of many, whereas on a scalar series it removes most of the
series.

\emph{The observer matters.} The result above is on the parameter norm. On the training loss, ten
of the twenty-six cells separate them, though the surrogate comparison passes in the $S_5$ run
only.

\emph{The two runs without weight decay have almost no dynamic range.} At this window the baseline
belongs to the configuration rather than to the run: on a standardised straight line \cref{eq:mg}
returns about $2.77$, close to the pre-transition level of every run. The parameter-norm log of the
modular run is straight to under a per cent of its own spread across the whole comparison interval,
so its estimate spans three thousandths of a component, against a range some three orders of
magnitude larger in the generalising runs. Its $D = 1.00$ therefore records that the observer
offered the estimator no local structure to respond to. The $S_5$ run is a weaker case of the same
thing.

The $D$ and quantile columns of these two runs may not, then, be treated as informative dimensional
negatives. The four generalising runs are untouched by this. So is the surrogate comparison, in
which each run is measured only against surrogates of itself.

\section{The Theiler exclusion}\label{app:theilerall}

\subsection{The exclusion cap at the twenty-component configuration}\label{app:theiler}

The rule this study asks for is $W_T = \max((E-1)\tau,\, t_{\mathrm{acf}})$: at least
the full span of a delay vector, so that two neighbours cannot be near each other merely because
their windows overlap. The implementation caps $W_T$ at $150$ samples.

At the eight-component configuration the embedding span is $76$, so the cap binds only where the
autocorrelation time exceeds $150$. That never happens on the constructed systems, whose
autocorrelation time is a few samples, but it happens on five of the seven parameter-norm records
of \cref{sec:grokking}. Those five were scored at $W_T = 150$ rather than at the value the rule
asks for, in the direction that inflates the estimate. At the twenty-component configuration the
span is $624$ and the cap binds unconditionally. It is why \cref{sec:digits} marks that result
exploratory.

both exclusions.

\emph{The value at $W_T = 0$ is the right one.} On a uniformly sampled curve
the nearest neighbours of a point are its immediate temporal neighbours, for which \cref{eq:mg}
returns $1.199$; the transient returns that value in every cell. The excess over unity is the
lattice bias of a deterministically sampled curve.

\emph{The exclusion removes the near neighbours, not the neighbours.} The estimator fills its
twenty slots at every exclusion; only the distances change. Before any exclusion the nearest
neighbours of a point lie thousands of samples away under the fast quasiperiodic drive, but six
samples away on the transient (\cref{fig:traces}). Excluding $150$ samples therefore costs the fast
drive almost none of its close pairs and the transient all of them (\cref{fig:exclusion}). The distances the transient retains are then
nearly equal, their log-ratio sum collapses, and the reciprocal in \cref{eq:mg} diverges. An
exponential decay with no optimiser and no data
behind it reproduces both ends.

\emph{The asymmetry is general; the value $29$ is not.} Recurrence is not always needed even here. Under the fast drive
$W_T = 0$ already returns $r$, because that case is not oversampled; it is the slow drive that the
exclusion repairs, its nearest neighbours lying some six hundred samples away. A transient also
need not have dimension one: a decaying \emph{oscillation} correctly returns $2.36$ at every
exclusion.

\paragraph{A recurrence-based diagnostic.} The ratio
$\hat d_{MG}(W_T{=}150)/\hat d_{MG}(W_T{=}20)$ is unity under both recurrent drives and above five
on the transient. Unlike the trend-crossing count, this ratio tests recurrence in the reconstructed space rather than
non-monotonicity of the series. That is the gap \cref{sec:diagnostics} leaves open, which
\cref{app:eos} shows to matter on real runs. The principled alternative is older than either: a
space--time separation plot \citep{provenzale1992distinguishing} shows directly how neighbour
distance depends on separation in time. Choosing the exclusion by such a measurement rather than by
a rule is the obvious improvement to the protocol.

\textbf{T} is the one-layer transformer of \citet{nanda2023progress}: width $128$, four heads, no

extended reruns of \cref{sec:pairs} use the stricter rule instead, requiring the threshold to hold
over a sustained block.

\section{Descent at the edge of stability}\label{app:eos}

Descent at the edge of stability \citep{cohen2021gradient} is gradient descent at a fixed rate: the
top Hessian eigenvalue sharpens until it reaches $2/\eta$ and then stays there, while the loss
descends over long stretches and not over short ones. Such a run is deterministic and is not a
plain transient, so an undriven trajectory could satisfy the conditions of \cref{sec:classes}.

\paragraph{Setting.} We run full-batch descent without weight decay on the quadratic perceptron of
\cref{app:runs}, over a range of learning rates and two seeds (\cref{tab:eos}). The log is
written at \emph{every} optimiser step, because the oscillation is the two-cycle of the unstable
mode and its period is a few steps. The sharpness is measured by power iteration on Hessian-vector
products, taking the largest \emph{algebraic} eigenvalue, because the Hessian is indefinite early
in training and plain power iteration would then return a negative sharpness.

\centering

\paragraph{Oscillation is not recurrence.} Eight runs reach the edge and all eight oscillate, the
loss rising on one step in two, but only five of them return close to states already passed
through. The return column of \cref{tab:eos} separates those two groups by a factor of ten with
nothing in between.

\paragraph{Performance of the diagnostics.} The band of \cref{sec:diagnostics} rejects all eight
runs at the edge, on the training loss and on the parameter norm alike, whether or not
they recur. Nor does the estimate separate the runs that return from those that do not: their
ranges on the training loss overlap. The trend-crossing count tests non-monotonicity and not
recurrence, so it passes every run at the edge, while $\rho_{\mathrm{ident}}$ stays far outside the
band in both groups.

\paragraph{Dependence on the logging stride.} Written at the stride every other full-batch log here
uses, most of the phenomenon disappears (\cref{fig:eos}): over these eight runs the median
trend-crossing count falls from $71$ to $6$. Whether a log looks like a transient is thus in part a
property of how often it was written.

\section{The ceiling: embedding dimension against record length}\label{app:ceiling}

\Cref{sec:ladder} reports that the estimator saturates above about eight components. Two
explanations were available: the embedding condition $E > 2d$ and the finite-record bound
$2\log_{10} N$ \citep{eckmann1992fundamental}. Each is insensitive to the variable the
other names, so we sweep $E_{\max}$ at a fixed record length and the record length at fixed $E_{\max}$,

leaving the delay lag its meaning in periods. Every trajectory is bit-identical to the published one
over their common prefix.

\paragraph{Outcome.} The level at high rank is set by the embedding: a sixty-fourfold increase in
the record adds $1.5$ components, whereas a sixfold increase in $E_{\max}$ adds eight. The tracking
ceiling follows neither variable. It never passes $11.2$, yet at a quarter of the record the rest
of the study uses it still tracks to $8.3$, where $2\log_{10}N$ allows only $6.6$. A bound set by the
record length cannot produce that, so \citet{eckmann1992fundamental} is not the explanation here.
Nor is $E > 2d$ in the form we used it: the ceiling rises by a tenth of a component per unit of

wrong at both ends.

\paragraph{A purely linear statistic reproduces the whole dependence.} Across the $E_{\max}$ sweep
$\PR_{\mathrm{delay}}$ rises over the same range as MG; across the record sweep it is flat; and a
single logarithm in $E_{\max}$ fits it more tightly than any form fits anything else in this
appendix. Most of the ceiling is therefore the number of components the delay window resolves
\emph{linearly}.

\paragraph{The unexplained tracking limit.} We cannot say why the tracking ceiling lies near eleven
components. It does not follow the measured effective rank, which equals $r$ throughout, nor does
any form we fitted account for it.

above the band at every record length, so a longer record never makes an unidentifiable estimate
identifiable; it falls into the band as $E_{\max}$ grows, at about the rank where tracking
stops.

\section{Two controls separating the estimator from roughness}\label{app:clocks}

Roughness is $\operatorname{std}(\Delta x)/\operatorname{std}(x)$. The controls below separate it
from the estimate in both directions, each given its pass criterion before it was run.

\emph{The number of components changes and the roughness does not.} Both systems in this control are a
sum of four sinusoids. In the first the four frequencies are rationally independent, so the path
lies on a four-dimensional torus and $d_{\mathrm{act}} = 4$. In the second they are harmonics of one
irrational base frequency: a single phase fixes all four sinusoids at once, the path is a circle,
and $d_{\mathrm{act}} = 1$. The base frequency of the second system is then set by bisection so
that the two have the \emph{same} roughness, to a part in $10^{15}$. A scheduled run passes between
the two through ramped envelopes, leaving unscored every window a ramp reaches into
(\cref{fig:switch}).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_switch.pdf}
\caption{A change in the number of independent phases, tracked as it happens. The harmonic control
of this appendix, scheduled from four phases to one and back; median over eight seeds, band their
range. Dashed in (a), the number of phases, drawn only where no ramp reaches into the window. The
linear statistic of (c) moves in the opposite direction.}
\label{fig:switch}
\end{figure}

\emph{The roughness changes and the number of components does not.} Five monotone observers
$g(x) = x + a x^{p}$ are applied to one four-phase system. Each is invertible, so it may bend the
recorded set but cannot add or remove a coordinate. Each also changes the local scale $g'(x)$ and
with it the spacing of consecutive samples.

Both criteria were fixed before the runs: that the estimate should follow the number of phases
while the roughness holds still, and that it should stay within a tenth of a component of four
while the roughness moves. \Cref{tab:clocks} shows both hold.

\paragraph{The limits of this control.} Both controls separate the statistics on a constructed
system whose phases are known. They say nothing about a training log, where the comparison against
surrogates in \cref{sec:matched} carries the claim instead.

Two findings count against the estimator. The linear participation ratio reads higher on the circle
than on the four-torus (\cref{tab:clocks}), so anyone treating it as a count of phases would order
the two backwards. On the circle the estimate is also not geometric: every neighbour surviving the
exclusion there is a sample from another pass around the circle. The local speed of the curve
multiplies all of a point's neighbour distances alike; speed cancels from the log-ratios of
\cref{eq:mg}. The
residue depends on the arithmetic of the base frequency: two of the eight seeds drew a frequency
whose multiples cluster into near-duplicates; their estimate is reproducible from the fractional
parts of those multiples alone, with no signal and no neighbour search.

\section{Compute cost and code availability}\label{app:compute}

\paragraph{Compute cost.} Everything here runs in a few hours on one consumer GPU and one desktop
CPU. The largest GPU item is the edge-of-stability experiment of \cref{app:eos},
which takes half an hour on a single T4; the analysis is the more expensive half and runs entirely
on CPU, a few core-hours per experiment.

\paragraph{Cost of the trajectory sketch.} At each logged step the sketch keeps $4096$ floats
against the full parameter vectors of \cref{app:runs}: a run whose raw trajectory would occupy $88$
megabytes occupies $2.5$. We train the same configuration with and without the sketch attached,
alternating the order so that warm-up cannot favour either. The measured difference is negative,
which is a bound rather than a result: at the logging stride the transformer runs use, the cost of the sketch lies below the
run-to-run variation of the machine. \texttt{sketch\_cost.py} records the measurement.

\paragraph{Code availability.} All code, configurations and result files behind every number and
figure in this paper will be released publicly on acceptance. The figures regenerate from the
committed result files with a single command.
